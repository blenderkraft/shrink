# Game Overview

Shrink is a short 2D comedy game about board-game collecting. You do not play the board games. You acquire them, keep them sealed, arrange them, post photos of them, and try to figure out which of those habits the hobby is actually rewarding.

It is one evening long. Four in-game months, then the game shows you the rules it was using and scores the shelf you built. A second run uses a different mix of rules. The joke is the same. The law is not.

The tone comes from [[Collector Tropes]]. On screen the sacred objects have joke names, so the game can ship: Patchwerk, Monopology, Unó, Brass: Birminghammer, Arks, Gloomhaven't. Everyone knows what they are. The reviewer is not The Dice Tower. He is Tom Barrel, and the show is The Dice Pile.

## What you are trying to do

You are trying to win at collecting.

The game tells you that up front, and then it lies about how. Your phone shows a number called Clout. It goes up when you do what a respectable hobbyist does: buy the hot game, open it, agree with the reviewer, keep Monopoly off the shelf. Clout moves immediately, with a little chime, every time you behave. It feels like the score. It is not the score.

The real score is hidden. Call it Canon. It is a handful of rules rolled at the start of the run and kept quiet until the end. Some of them are stable, so the game can teach you. Some of them change between runs, so a walkthrough goes stale.

The stable ones are the premise:

- Several copies of Patchwerk score more than one. The first is ordinary. The fourth is a personality. Opening any of them ruins the set.
- Owning Monopology loses points. Owning another edition loses more. Owning none does not pay you. Absence is not a hobby.
- Exactly five Unó variants can be worth a lot. Clout hates Unó the entire time. That contradiction is how the game admits Clout is a liar.

Other rules might be live or might not. A sealed Brass on the shelf. Exactly one empty cube, because the games need room to breathe. Agreeing with Tom. Filming your own channel. A full shelf of still-sealed games. You do not get a list. You get a shelf, a budget, and four months.

So the goal is two goals braided together. Build the collection the hidden rules want. Learn those rules early enough that the last months are on purpose. A player who chases Clout will look successful and lose. A player who buys randomly will sometimes luck into a joke and still lose, because they cannot repeat it. The win is recognizing the dumb religion and joining it before the money runs out.

There is no story to complete and no relationship to max. At the end you are shown your number, the rules, and the purchase that defined you. That roast is the ending. A high score is bragging rights on the next run, not a cutscene.

## Where you are

You play from a desk in a small room. The camera does not move and you do not walk. The room is one picture: a Kallax covering the wall, a laptop, a TV.

Click the wall and you are arranging games. Click the laptop and the computer fills the screen. Click the TV and The Dice Pile plays. Click the room, or press Esc, and you are back at the desk. That is the whole map.

Nothing is a puzzle door. If you can see it, you can use it. The game is what you choose to do with the same three things, over and over, while the shelf gets worse.

## What you do

Dragging is free. You can fuss with the shelf all month. Consequential moves spend money or spend the month's attention. You get a small pile of attention each month, enough for a handful of real decisions, not enough to do everything.

**Buy.** On the laptop, open the shop or a Kickstarter page and purchase a game. It arrives sealed. Kickstarter takes your money now and the box next month, which is the point. All-in adds a deluxe sticker: metal coins, a bigger box, a promo you will not open. You can buy a second, third, and fourth copy of the same game. The shop will not stop you. Clout will not applaud you. Canon might.

**Arrange.** Drag boxes into cubes. Leave a cube empty on purpose. Turn on the cube lights. Sleeve a game. Put an archival bag over the shrink. Stack two boxes in one cube and watch the lids drift. These are not busywork. Empty space, lights, and bags are the kind of thing the hidden rules notice, and the comments will talk about them as if they were moral choices.

**Open.** Peel the shrink. The plastic comes off, the box scuffs, and the game claims you had a great time. It takes a few seconds. You never play a board game inside Shrink. Opening is a confession you can make for Clout, and a way to ruin a sealed set if Canon wanted it sealed.

**Post.** Photograph the wall and put it on the subreddit. The comments arrive in the voice of the hobby: where is Brass, why is that cube empty, are you okay, one of us. Some of that is noise. Some of it is a rule, said sideways. You can post once a month. The photo is saved into an album whether the comments are kind or not.

**Watch.** Tom Barrel reviews two or three games in the shop. Twenty seconds. He describes the components, says you will like it if you like games, holds up a few fingers, and reads a sponsor line for a shelf insert. Buying what he praised raises Clout. Whether it raises Canon is one of the things you are there to find out.

**Mail him a copy.** A game leaves the shelf for a month. It comes back still sealed, in a bag, with his finger count written on the plastic. You spent a cube and a month to own a sticker.

**Start a channel.** The same TV, your name in the corner. You pick a format: fingers and a sponsor, a two-handed run-through that tells people to buy it, or a stylish review that tells them not to. Filming spends the month's attention. The comments tell you, indirectly, whether becoming the channel was the hobby or a way of hiding from a table.

**Sell or cull.** List a game. Selling a sealed exclusive for more than you paid looks clever and may be scored as a tell. Giving one away looks virtuous and may be scored as a different tell.

**Answer or ignore.** Once a month someone writes you. A text from your spouse, a pledge-manager email, a comment that will not drop it. You can do what they ask, usually free up a cube or skip a purchase, or you can buy anyway. Ignoring them is always allowed. It is not always free.

**Test.** A few times in the whole run, not every month, you can force a narrow answer. Ask the sub, check a burner account, email the reviewer. The game tells you what the last purchase did to the hidden score: up a lot, up a little, nothing, down. It does not show the rule. Spend these on the weird buys. Wasting one on "did opening a game give me Clout" teaches you nothing you could not already see.

**Take notes.** The laptop has a notes app. You pin theories in your own words, short ones: "second Patchwerk matters," "Tom is a trap," "empty cube is the point." At the end, a theory that matches a live rule pays extra. A theory that is the opposite of a live rule costs you. Writing nothing is safer than being confidently normal, and worse than being right. The notes are how experimenting becomes a strategy instead of a vibe.

Money is loose on purpose. You can go into debt. Past a point the spouse texts start early, and the hidden rules that care about debt wake up. There is no energy bar, no skill tree, and no crafting. The budget and the monthly attention are the only brakes.

## A month

1. The TV has a new upload. Watch it or don't. The shop highlights whatever Tom talked about.
2. You spend attention. Buy, pledge, open, post, film, test, cull. Between those, you drag the shelf into the shape you are willing to be seen with.
3. One message arrives that knows something. It might name a rule in character ("the games need room to breathe, you said so yourself") or it might only show you a consequence. You answer it or you don't.
4. The month ends. The wall is photographed for the album. Unfinished Kickstarters stay in the mail. Next month's hotness list is already wrong.

By month four you should have heard about half the rules out loud and be gambling on the rest. If you still have no idea, you ignored the comments and spent the tests on obvious moves. The game will still score you. It will just be rude about it.

## How you know you're doing well

You don't, not really. That is the game.

Clout is a liar with good manners. A rising phone number means you are doing what the timeline rewards. The timeline is not the judge.

Better signals, all of them incomplete:

- A comment that is too specific. "Three Patchwerks and none of them open. Coward, but correct." That is closer to a rule than a joke, or it is a joke that happens to be the rule.
- A Test result that surprises you. If the fourth copy went up a lot and the first copy did not, stop buying variety.
- Your own notes. If two months of pins all point the same direction, spend month three as if they are true. Being wrong on purpose still teaches you. Dabbling does not.
- The spouse, the subreddit, and Tom do not agree. They are three witnesses. Only some of them are under oath in this run. When they contradict, believe the one whose last tip matched a Test.

The UI never shows +4. If a number appears before the ending, it is Clout, a price, a finger count, or a debt. Those are props.

## The ending

The album opens. Four shelfies, then the live rules in plain language, then your total. The game circles the purchase that scored the most and the one that cost the most, and it quotes the comment or text that tried to tell you.

A good ending is specific. Your fourth Patchwerk was the whole personality. You opened the Monopology, which helped neither score. You agreed with Tom every month, and this run Tom was a shill. You left one cube empty and the game thanks you for letting the boxes express themselves.

A bad ending is a cutscene. The camera stays on the shelf.

New Game keeps the Patchwerk ladder, the Monopology penalty, and the five-Unó trap, and reshuffles the rest. Those three are the tutorial. They are also the joke.

## What this game is not

It is not a board game, and it is not a video game about playing board games. Opening a box does not drop you into Patchwork.

It is not a 3D apartment and not a point-and-click adventure. You do not walk, and you do not solve your way into new rooms. The detail is the shrink, the scuff, the bag, the drifting lid, and the browser. Eight covers are drawn so the jokes are recognizable. The rest of the catalog is a colored box with a title. Thirty games is enough.

It is not a dating sim, and the spouse is not the punchline. They are a person with a name who has to live with the wall. The jokes land on you.

It is one room, one laptop, one TV, four months. If a feature needs a new place or a new verb, it does not belong in the first version.
